## Abstract

The decimal repetend of $1/7$, the six-digit number $142857$, is the best-known
**cyclic number**: its cyclic rotations are its first six multiples. The
mechanism is the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$ and
the fact that $10$ is a primitive root modulo $7$. Primes with the analogous
property are called **full reptend primes**. Whether infinitely many exist in
base $10$ is a special case of **Artin's conjecture on primitive roots**, which
Christopher Hooley proved in 1967 under the **Generalized Riemann Hypothesis
(GRH)** — not under the classical Riemann Hypothesis alone.

The same adjective, *cyclic*, also appears in **cyclic error-correcting codes**
and in **cryptography based on the discrete logarithm problem**. These are
genuine uses of cyclic groups, but they are different groups, and the
number-theoretic results do not transfer to a Hamming decoder or to
elliptic-curve security. This note separates the one real number-theoretic
chain from the shared vocabulary, and records what is proved, what is
conditional, and what is open.

## 0. Scope and status labels

Popular accounts often mix four kinds of statement. To keep them apart, key
claims below are tagged with one of these labels:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Definition** | A convention or name; neither true nor false. |
| **Theorem** | Proved, unconditionally. |
| **Conjecture** | Open; supported by evidence and heuristics. |
| **Conditional** | Proved from an unproved hypothesis, such as GRH. |
| **Analogy** | A structural resemblance; it implies nothing by itself. |

Two distinctions matter throughout:

1. **RH versus GRH.** Classical RH concerns the zeros of the Riemann zeta
   function $\zeta(s)$. GRH concerns the zeros of Dirichlet $L$-functions and,
   more generally, Dedekind zeta functions of number fields. RH does not imply
   GRH.
2. **Four meanings of "cyclic".** A cyclic *number* is a repeating-digit block
   in a given base; a cyclic *group* is an algebraic object; a cyclic *code* is
   a shift-invariant linear code; a cyclic *subgroup* is a subgroup generated by
   one element. These notions are related by group theory, but they are not the
   same object.

## 1. The number 142857

### 1.1 The cyclic property

The defining property is visible in the multiplication table:

| $k$ | $k \times 142857$ | left rotation of $142857$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 142857 | 0 places |
| 2 | 285714 | 2 places |
| 3 | 428571 | 1 place |
| 4 | 571428 | 4 places |
| 5 | 714285 | 5 places |
| 6 | 857142 | 3 places |

Each product is a cyclic rotation of the original block, and each of the six
rotations occurs exactly once. A number with this property is a **cyclic
number** in the given base. The six-digit number $142857$ is the smallest
nontrivial decimal example.

The order of the multipliers is not the order of the rotations. The rotation
by $r$ places corresponds to multiplication by $10^r \bmod 7$; the multiplier
$k$ is therefore $10^r \bmod 7$. For example, a left rotation by one place
gives $428571 = 3 \times 142857$, and $10 \equiv 3 \pmod 7$.

### 1.2 Why the pattern holds

The block is the repeating part of $1/7$:

$$
\frac{1}{7} = 0.\overline{142857}.
$$

Long division makes the mechanism explicit. At each step, the current
remainder is multiplied by $10$ and divided by $7$:

| step | digit | remainder |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 4 | 2 |
| 3 | 2 | 6 |
| 4 | 8 | 4 |
| 5 | 5 | 5 |
| 6 | 7 | 1 |

The remainders are $3,2,6,4,5,1$. These are the nonzero residues
$10^k \bmod 7$, read in the order generated by $10$ starting at $k=1$. The
cycle closes because $10^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$.

This generalises. Let $b \ge 2$ be a base, let $p$ be a prime with $p \nmid b$,
and let

$$
L = \mathrm{ord}_p(b)
$$

be the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$. Then $1/p$ is purely periodic
in base $b$ with period $L$. When $L = p-1$, the prime $p$ is called a **full
reptend prime** in base $b$, and the repetend

$$
R = \frac{b^{p-1}-1}{p}
$$

is a cyclic number.

**Theorem (the reptend is cyclic).** Let $p$ be a full reptend prime in base
$b$, let $L = p-1$, and let $R = (b^L-1)/p$. Then the $L$ cyclic rotations of
the $L$-digit base $b$ block of $R$ are precisely

$$
R,\ 2R,\ 3R,\ \dots,\ (p-1)R,
$$

each occurring once.

*Proof sketch.* For $0 \le k < L$, write $b^k = qp + m_k$ with
$1 \le m_k \le p-1$. Then

$$
b^k R = qpR + m_kR = q(b^L-1) + m_kR.
$$

Because $0 < m_kR < pR = b^L-1$, the term $m_kR$ is the least nonnegative
residue of $b^kR$ modulo $b^L-1$. Multiplication by $b$ modulo $b^L-1$ is
exactly a cyclic shift of the base $b$ digits, so that residue is the rotation
of $R$ by $k$ places. As $k$ runs from $0$ to $L-1$, the residues $m_k$ run
through $1,\dots,p-1$ because $b$ is a primitive root. $\square$

As a check, $7 \times 142857 = 999999 = 10^6-1$.

The converse is classical: a nontrivial base $b$ cyclic number of length $L$
arises only when $L+1$ is a full reptend prime in base $b$ (with the usual
exclusion of single-digit and repeated-block cases). The cyclic property is
therefore not a coincidence of the digits of $142857$; it is the shadow of the
group $(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$.

### 1.3 Full reptend primes in base 10

By definition, $p$ is full reptend in base $10$ exactly when $10$ is a
primitive root modulo $p$. The first few are

$$
7,\ 17,\ 19,\ 23,\ 29,\ 47,\ 59,\ 61,\ 97,\ 109,\ 113,\ 131,\ 149,\ 167,\ 179,\dots
$$

(OEIS [A001913](https://oeis.org/A001913)). The prime $13$ is a useful
counterexample: $1/13 = 0.\overline{076923}$ has period $6$, not $12$, so $13$
is not full reptend.

The project computes all of this directly:

```python
from modcode.number_theory.full_reptend import (
    cyclic_multiples,
    full_reptend_cycle,
    full_reptend_primes,
    multiplicative_order,
)

multiplicative_order(10, 7)       # 6
multiplicative_order(10, 13)      # 6, not 12
full_reptend_cycle(7)             # '142857'
full_reptend_cycle(17)            # '0588235294117647'
full_reptend_primes(100)          # (7, 17, 19, 23, 29, 47, 59, 61, 97)
cyclic_multiples(7)[:3]           # ('142857', '285714', '428571')
```

These are finite computations. They verify particular primes; they do not prove
that infinitely many exist.

### 1.4 Why 142857 is the only one without a leading zero

For $p \ge 11$,

$$
R = \frac{10^{p-1}-1}{p} < 10^{p-2},
$$

so the $(p-1)$-digit block of $1/p$ begins with $0$. The only full reptend
prime below $11$ is $7$, and its block $142857$ has six digits with no leading
zero. Hence:

> **Theorem.** Without allowing leading zeros, $142857$ is the only nontrivial
> cyclic number in base $10$.

Allow leading zeros and the sequence continues with the blocks of $1/17$,
$1/19$, $1/23$, and so on:

$$
\frac{10^{16}-1}{17} = 0588235294117647,
\qquad
\frac{10^{18}-1}{19} = 052631578947368421.
$$

The leading zero is bookkeeping, not a defect: the rotation property acts on
fixed-width digit strings, and dropping the zero would make some rotations look
like shorter numbers.

### 1.5 What is open

Given a prime $p$, deciding whether it is full reptend is a finite computation:
factor $p-1$ and check that $10^{(p-1)/q} \not\equiv 1 \pmod p$ for every prime
$q \mid p-1$. The open question is not "is this prime full reptend?" but
"**are there infinitely many?**" That is Artin's conjecture.

## 2. Artin's conjecture and the Riemann Hypothesis

### 2.1 Artin's conjecture

Artin's conjecture on primitive roots (1927) states that if $a$ is an integer
that is neither a perfect square nor $-1$, then $a$ is a primitive root modulo
infinitely many primes $p$. The conjecture also predicts an asymptotic density.

Write $a = a_0 s^2$ with $a_0$ squarefree. Under the additional conditions that
$a$ is not a perfect power and $a_0 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the predicted density
is **Artin's constant**

$$
C_{\mathrm{Artin}}
= \prod_{q\ \text{prime}} \left(1 - \frac{1}{q(q-1)}\right)
= 0.3739558136\ldots
$$

For $a = 10$, the hypotheses hold: $10 = 2 \cdot 5$ is squarefree, is not a
perfect power, and $10 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$. So Artin predicts that full reptend
primes in base $10$ have density $C_{\mathrm{Artin}}$ among all primes, and in
particular that there are infinitely many.

**Status: conjecture.** No specific value of $a$ has been proved to satisfy
Artin's conjecture unconditionally. This includes $a=10$.

### 2.2 Hooley's conditional theorem

Christopher Hooley proved in 1967 that **GRH implies Artin's conjecture**,
including the density formula [Hooley 1967]. This is the precise source of the
"Riemann Hypothesis" connection.

The relevant hypothesis is GRH, not classical RH. GRH states that for every
Dirichlet character $\chi$, every nontrivial zero of the Dirichlet
$L$-function $L(s,\chi)$ has real part $1/2$. It extends the same assertion to
Dedekind zeta functions of number fields. Hooley's argument uses GRH for a
family of Kummer extensions $\mathbb{Q}(a^{1/q}, \zeta_q)$, one for each prime
$q$.

**Status: conditional theorem.** GRH $\Rightarrow$ Artin, for each fixed
admissible $a$. It is not known unconditionally.

### 2.3 RH is not GRH

This is the most common slip in popular accounts:

> **Classical RH** is the assertion about the zeros of $\zeta(s)$.
> **GRH** is the assertion about the zeros of all Dirichlet $L$-functions
> (and Dedekind zeta functions).

Classical RH is the special case $\chi = 1$. RH does not imply GRH. Therefore
Hooley's theorem does not say "RH implies Artin"; it says "GRH implies Artin".
A proof of RH alone would not settle Artin's conjecture.

The distinction is not pedantic. The two statements have different quantifier
structure over families of $L$-functions, and the error terms Hooley needs are
precisely the ones GRH supplies for the relevant Kummer extensions.

### 2.4 Why GRH is enough

The condition that $a$ is a primitive root modulo $p$ can be checked prime by
prime: for every prime $q \mid p-1$, $a$ must not be a $q$-th power modulo $p$.
For fixed $q$, the primes $p$ for which $a$ is a $q$-th power are governed by
splitting conditions in the Kummer extension $\mathbb{Q}(a^{1/q}, \zeta_q)$.
Counting such primes is a Chebotarev-type problem. GRH provides effective
error terms for the prime ideal theorem in these fields, strong enough to sum
the resulting inclusion–exclusion over all $q$ and obtain a positive density.

This is also why the connection to RH is *indirect*. RH controls the error term
in the prime number theorem for $\zeta(s)$; GRH controls the analogous error
terms for the arithmetic progressions and number fields that encode the
primitive-root condition.

### 2.5 What is known unconditionally

- **Heath-Brown (1986)** proved unconditionally that Artin's conjecture can
  fail for at most two prime values of $a$ [Heath-Brown 1986]. The proof does
  not identify the exceptions, and it does not settle any particular $a$.
- **Finite verification** has confirmed the pattern for enormous ranges of
  primes. Finite evidence is not a proof of infinitude.
- **Lenstra (1977)** and others clarified the density corrections for
  non-squarefree and perfect-power $a$ [Lenstra 1977]; Murty's survey is a
  standard entry point [Murty 1988].

A useful numerical check is to count full reptend primes up to a bound. With
$10{,}000$ as the bound, the project finds $467$ full reptend primes among
$1{,}229$ primes, a ratio of $0.379984$. This is close to
$C_{\mathrm{Artin}} = 0.373956$, but a finite prefix is not a limit and the
count is not a proof.

### 2.6 The precise chain

The number-theoretic chain is:

```
full reptend prime p          (definition)
  ⇔ ord_p(10) = p − 1         (equivalent condition)
  ⇒ the repetend is a cyclic number   (theorem, §1.2)

infinitely many such p        (Artin's conjecture for a = 10)
  ⇐ GRH                       (Hooley 1967, conditional)
```

Read the arrows carefully. The first two lines are unconditional mathematics.
The last line is a conditional existence theorem. Nothing in this chain says
that the decimal digits of $1/p$ are statistically random, normal, or
cryptographically useful. The cyclic property is a statement about rotations
and multiples, not about digit frequencies.

### 2.7 The quasi-Riemann Hypothesis: what it does and does not imply

In October 2026, OpenAI released preprints claiming a **quasi-Riemann
hypothesis** (QRH): a fixed zero-free half-plane for all finite-order Hecke
$L$-functions over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, and hence for all Dirichlet
$L$-functions. The first version used $\Re s \le 11/12$; a stronger version uses
$\Re s \le 7/8$, and Xu and Sang refine the boundary to $0.8748867$ under the
source hypotheses [OpenAI 2026; Xu & Sang 2026]. Quanta reported that human
experts had unpacked the argument [Quanta 2026]. It is still a very recent
preprint claim, and Visser notes that some of the OpenAI preprints have been
withdrawn or corrected [Visser 2026].

The shape of the statement matters. From $\Re\rho \le 11/12$ and the
functional equation, one gets $\Re\rho \in [1/12,11/12]$; the stronger claim
gives $[1/8,7/8]$. That is a **containing strip**, not a zero-free strip: it
excludes zeros near $0$ and $1$, but it says nothing about the critical line. It
is not “RH with a wider strip”; it is a zero-free region near the edge of the
critical strip.

Hooley's theorem is conditional on GRH: zeros on $\Re s = 1/2$ for the relevant
$L$-functions. QRH does not imply GRH and does not supply that control. A
zero-free half-plane at $7/8$ would give, for fixed modulus, a prime number
theorem in arithmetic progressions with the power-saving error
$O(x^{7/8+o(1)})$. That is a strong statement — for large $x$ it is smaller than
the main term $x/\log x$ — but it is still not GRH, and the QRH claim covers
Dirichlet $L$-functions and a specific Hecke family over
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, not the Kummer extensions and the uniform sum over $q$
that Hooley's argument uses.

There is also a correction to a common shorthand. QRH does not give the
asymptotic

$$
P_b(x) = C_b\,\frac{x}{\log x} +
O\!\left(x e^{-c\sqrt{\log x}}\right).
$$

The constant $C_b$ and the asymptotic itself are the content of Artin's
conjecture; a zero-free half-plane alone does not produce them. A $7/8$
zero-free half-plane would give a power-saving $O(x^{7/8+o(1)})$ (for fixed
modulus), not the subexponential form displayed above. The subexponential form
is weaker than the power saving for large $x$, but neither is a proof of the
Artin asymptotic.

What QRH would provide is a stronger unconditional analytic input for prime
distribution than the classical zero-free regions. Whether it can be extended
to the family and uniformity needed for Artin's primitive-root conjecture is a
separate question. The status of the cyclic-number chain is unchanged:

$$
\text{full reptend primes}
\;\Longleftarrow\; \text{Artin}
\;\Longleftarrow\; \text{GRH (Hooley)}.
$$

A terminology caution: Artin's primitive-root conjecture is not the Artin
$L$-function conjecture. Hooley's proof uses GRH for Kummer extensions; it is
not a statement about the Artin $L$-functions of a Galois representation.
“Artin $L$-functions” and “metaplectic forms” should not be used to identify the
two.

### 2.8 Residual-index densities

For a prime $p \nmid 10$, the residual index is

$$
c_p = \frac{p-1}{\mathrm{ord}_p(10)}.
$$

Artin's conjecture is the case $c_p=1$ (full period). The case $c_p=m$ is a
prescribed index, not the coding-theory notion of a “semi-cyclic” code; the
word is overloaded. Under GRH, the density is the Hooley–Lenstra field-degree
sum

$$
\delta(10,m)
= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{\mu(n)}
{\left[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{mn}, 10^{1/(mn)}) : \mathbb{Q}\right]}.
$$

For squarefree $10$, the degree is $mn\,\phi(mn)$, except when
$\sqrt{10}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{mn})$ — equivalently $40 \mid mn$ — where it
drops by a factor of two.

The small values are:

| $m$ | density | value |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | $A$ | 0.3739558 |
| 2 | $3A/4$ | 0.2804669 |
| 4 | $29A/152$ | 0.0713468 |

The frequently quoted $29A/52\approx0.2085$ is not consistent with the
field-degree formula or with direct counting. At $X=10^6$, the observed
fractions are $0.3758, 0.2805, 0.0719$ for $m=1,2,4$; at $X=5\cdot10^6$
the $m=4$ fraction is $0.07082$, approaching $0.07135$. The $29/152$
denominator comes from adding the $10\mid n$ quadratic correction
$A/304$ to the base sum $3A/16$.

The densities fall with $m$ in this range roughly quadratically for $m\ge2$
— $0.2805\to0.0713\to0.0166$ for $m=2,4,8$ — but that is an empirical
observation from the table, not a proof of an asymptotic. The finite counts
are unconditional but converge slowly; the GRH formula is the practical tool.
`modcode.number_theory.residual_index` computes the sum and the counts;
`examples/residual_index_lab.py` plots both.

## 3. Cyclic codes and forward error correction

### 3.1 Definition

Let $\mathbb{F}_q$ be a finite field and let $C \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n$ be a
linear code of length $n$. The cyclic shift is

$$
\sigma(c_0,c_1,\dots,c_{n-1}) = (c_{n-1},c_0,\dots,c_{n-2}).
$$

The code $C$ is **cyclic** if $\sigma(C) = C$: every cyclic shift of every
codeword is again a codeword. Equivalently, $C$ is invariant under the cyclic
group $C_n$ generated by $\sigma$.

### 3.2 The polynomial model

Identify a word with a polynomial:

$$
(c_0,\dots,c_{n-1}) \longleftrightarrow c(x) = c_0 + c_1x + \cdots + c_{n-1}x^{n-1}
\quad \in \quad R = \mathbb{F}_q[x]/(x^n-1).
$$

Under this identification, multiplication by $x$ is the cyclic shift. A cyclic
code is therefore an ideal of $R$. Since $R$ is a principal ideal ring, every
cyclic code has the form

$$
C = \langle g(x) \rangle, \qquad g(x) \mid x^n-1,
$$

where $g$ is the unique monic generator polynomial of minimal degree. If
$\deg g = n-k$, the code has dimension $k$. Encoding can be polynomial
multiplication $c(x) = m(x)g(x)$, or systematic encoding via the remainder of
$x^{n-k}m(x)$ modulo $g(x)$. The syndrome of a received word $r(x)$ is
$s(x) = r(x) \bmod g(x)$.

When $\gcd(n,q)=1$, the ring $R$ is semisimple and $x^n-1$ splits into
distinct irreducible factors over a suitable extension field. This is the
setting for BCH and Reed–Solomon codes, whose design distances are read from
the roots of $g$ in $\mathbb{F}_{q^m}$.

### 3.3 Hamming(7,4)

The binary $[7,4,3]$ Hamming code has the cyclic representation

$$
g(x) = x^3 + x + 1, \qquad
x^7-1 = (x+1)(x^3+x+1)(x^3+x^2+1) \quad \text{over } \mathbb{F}_2.
$$

The code has $2^4 = 16$ codewords, minimum distance $3$, and corrects one bit
error. It is perfect: the $16$ radius-one Hamming balls partition
$\mathbb{F}_2^7$, since $16 \cdot (1+7) = 128 = 2^7$.

The project implements the systematic version in `modcode.fec.hamming`:

```python
from modcode.fec.hamming import Hamming74

code = Hamming74()
code.encode([1, 0, 1, 0])   # array([1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1], dtype=uint8)
```

The test suite checks that every codeword is closed under all seven cyclic
shifts (`tests/test_cyclic_fec.py`). It also checks a subtle negative result:
shortening the code by fixing one coordinate destroys six-bit cyclic closure.
That is the correct lesson — **shortening and cyclic structure do not
generally commute**.

### 3.4 Cyclic, quasi-cyclic, shortened, and "semi-cyclic"

The standard families are precise:

| Family | Property | Standard term? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclic | invariant under all $n$ coordinate shifts | yes | Hamming(7,4) |
| Quasi-cyclic, index $s$ | invariant under shifts by multiples of $s$ | yes | QC-LDPC |
| Shortened | subcode with some coordinates fixed to zero, then deleted | yes | shortened Reed–Solomon |
| Punctured | some coordinates deleted | yes | rate-compatible codes |
| Constacyclic | shift followed by a scalar multiplication | yes | constacyclic codes |
| "Semi-cyclic" | varies by source | **no** | some patent literature |

The term **"semi-cyclic" has no single standard definition** in coding theory.
It appears in some patent literature for codes with a restricted rotation
property, but the restriction must be stated explicitly. It is not a synonym
for "quasi-cyclic", and it is not a synonym for "shortened". If a document uses
the term, the first question should be: *which shift subgroup is the code
invariant under, and on which coordinates?*

The project's `tests/test_cyclic_fec.py` makes the shortened-code point
concrete: after shortening, a rotated codeword can leave the shortened set.
That is expected, not a bug. A shortened code is a subcode with fixed zero
coordinates; a shift moves those zeros, so the shifted word generally lies
outside the subcode.

### 3.5 Which cyclic group?

A cyclic code is invariant under the **coordinate-shift group** $C_n$, of order
$n$. A full reptend prime involves the **multiplicative group**
$(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$, of order $p-1$. These are different
groups.

The coincidence of the number $7$ makes the distinction easy to see:

- Hamming(7,4) is invariant under $C_7$, a cyclic group of order $7$.
- $1/7$ has period $6$ because $(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})^{\times} \cong C_6$.

$C_7$ and $C_6$ are not isomorphic. The shared $7$ is decorative. In a BCH or
Reed–Solomon code there is a third cyclic group in play: the multiplicative
group $\mathbb{F}_{q^m}^{\times}$ of an extension field, where the roots of the
generator polynomial live. It is cyclic, but it is not the shift group either.

So "cyclic code" and "cyclic number" share an adjective and a general
algebraic theme, not a common group.

### 3.6 Cyclic topology in quantum error correction

A different use of “cyclic” appears in quantum stabilizer codes. There the
codewords are subspaces of $(\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n}$, and the stabilizer
group is an abelian subgroup of the Pauli group. A **cyclic topology** means
that the physical qubits are arranged on a ring and the stabilizer generators
are cyclic permutations of a base generator. Simakov and Besedin use this idea
to scale the five-qubit perfect code: for distances $d=3,5,7,9$, the base
stabilizer is repeated around a ring of alternating data and ancilla qubits,
and the total physical-qubit count scales linearly as $N=8d-14$. They give an
iSWAP/SWAP-based syndrome-extraction circuit and an LSTM decoder supported by
a lookup-table decoder, and report exponential suppression of the logical
error rate in a phenomenological noise model [Simakov & Besedin 2025].

This is a structural cousin of the classical cyclic code, not an instance of
the theorem that a cyclic code is an ideal in $\mathbb{F}_q[C_n]$. The
classical code is a subspace of $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ invariant under coordinate
shifts; the quantum code is a subspace of a tensor-product Hilbert space
stabilized by Pauli operators, and “cyclic” refers to the ring layout and the
cyclic permutation of stabilizer supports. The shared design principle is
symmetry; the algebraic objects and the error model are different.

### 3.7 CRC and quasi-cyclic codes in post-quantum cryptography

A CRC is a systematic cyclic code used for detection: the parity bits are the
remainder of $x^r m(x)$ modulo a generator $g(x)$. A degree $r$ CRC
detects every single-bit error and every burst whose length does not exceed
$r$, but it does not correct them [Peterson & Brown 1961].

QC-LDPC and QC-MDPC use the same block-circulant idea. In a QC-MDPC
McEliece-style scheme, the parity check is $H = [H_0 \mid H_1]$ with
circulant blocks, and the public generator is $G = [I \mid Q]$ with
$Q = (H_1^{-1} H_0)^T$, also circulant. A dense $Q$ needs $n^2$ bits; a
circulant $Q$ is determined by its first row, so $n$ bits suffice. The
private sparse blocks are described by their nonzero positions. This is the
key-size argument that makes code-based post-quantum proposals attractive
[Misoczki et al. 2013; BIKE].

The security assumption is the hardness of decoding a random quasi-cyclic
code, not the circulant structure by itself. The structure is both an
efficiency feature and a cryptanalytic target: highly structured codes can be
weaker than random ones, so parameter and decoder design matter. BIKE is the
canonical QC-MDPC example; HQC is another quasi-cyclic code-based KEM, but its
construction is not simply “QC-LDPC,” so that shorthand should not be read as a
precise classification. The toy `modcode.pqc.QCMDPC` demonstrates the layout,
key-size scaling, and a small bit-flipping decoder; its parameters are far too
small to be secure, and it is not BIKE.

The `modcode.pqc.analysis` layer makes the tradeoff measurable. The
bit-flipping success rate falls as the injected error weight rises; the
syndrome search space $\log_2 \binom{2n}{t}$ grows exponentially with $t$;
and the dual code’s low-weight spectrum contains the sparse secret rows, which
is the target of key-recovery attacks. Those are three quantities a real
parameter set must balance. The toy sizes are chosen to make the plots
readable, not to recommend parameters.

## 4. Cyclic groups in cryptography

### 4.1 The discrete logarithm problem

Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ be a cyclic group of order $n$. The **discrete
logarithm problem** (DLP) is: given $h \in G$, find $x$ such that

$$
h = g^x.
$$

Diffie–Hellman key exchange uses two exponents:

$$
A = g^a, \qquad B = g^b, \qquad K = B^a = A^b = g^{ab}.
$$

The security of the protocol rests on the assumed hardness of computing
$g^{ab}$ from $g^a$ and $g^b$ (the computational Diffie–Hellman assumption), or
on distinguishing it from random (the decisional version). These are
computational assumptions; they are not theorems, and they are not consequences
of RH or GRH.

In the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$, which is
cyclic of order $p-1$, practical systems use a prime-order subgroup of order
$q \mid p-1$, often with a safe prime $p = 2q+1$. The toy implementation in
`modcode.cryptography.toy_dh` uses the full group and a primitive root:

```python
from modcode.cryptography.toy_dh import ToyDiffieHellman

dh = ToyDiffieHellman(prime=23, generator=5)   # 5 is a primitive root mod 23
alice_public = dh.public_key(6)                # 8
bob_public = dh.public_key(15)                 # 19
dh.shared_secret(6, bob_public)                # 2
dh.shared_secret(15, alice_public)             # 2
```

It is deliberately insecure and exists only to show the group arithmetic.

### 4.2 Elliptic curves: subgroups, not always cyclic

For an elliptic curve $E$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$, the set $E(\mathbb{F}_q)$ of
points is a finite abelian group, but it need not be cyclic. Its structure is

$$
E(\mathbb{F}_q) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n_1\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n_2\mathbb{Z},
\qquad n_1 \mid n_2, \qquad n_1 \mid q-1.
$$

Elliptic-curve cryptography uses a prime-order subgroup $\langle P \rangle$ of
order $n$, with cofactor $h = \#E(\mathbb{F}_q)/n$. The security assumption is
the hardness of the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP) in that
subgroup. Saying "ECC is based on a cyclic group" is a useful shorthand, but
the precise statement is "a prime-order subgroup of a finite abelian group".

For generic groups, the best known discrete-logarithm algorithms (baby-step
giant-step, Pollard's rho) take $O(\sqrt{n})$ group operations. Finite-field
groups also have index-calculus algorithms with subexponential complexity.
No subexponential ECDLP algorithm is known for well-chosen curves.

### 4.3 What GRH does — and does not — do for cryptography

GRH is **not** a cryptographic hardness assumption. It is a tool in analytic
and algorithmic number theory. It is used, for example, to obtain conditional
bounds on the least primitive root modulo $p$, to analyse deterministic
primality tests (Miller's test is polynomial-time under GRH), and to sharpen
effective Chebotarev-type error terms.

None of this means that the security of Diffie–Hellman or ECC is conditional
on GRH. A proof of GRH would not yield a discrete-logarithm algorithm. It would
improve some bounds used in parameter selection and security analysis; it would
not break the systems. Conversely, a weakness in a deployed system would come
from a weak group, a bad implementation, a side channel, or a new algorithm —
not from the truth value of RH.

### 4.4 Quantum computing, not RH

The known serious threat to both finite-field DH and ECC is Shor's quantum
algorithm, which solves the discrete logarithm problem in polynomial time on a
sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer. That is a change of
computational model, not a consequence of RH or GRH. Post-quantum cryptography
is a response to Shor, not to the Riemann Hypothesis.

## 5. Synthesis: a shared vocabulary, not a single theorem chain

### 5.1 Four meanings of "cyclic"

| Expression | Object | Group or ring | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclic number $142857$ | decimal repetend of $1/7$ | $(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$ | theorem |
| Full reptend primes | primes with $\mathrm{ord}_p(10)=p-1$ | $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$ | infinitude open |
| Cyclic code | shift-invariant linear code | $C_n$ acting on $\mathbb{F}_q^n$; ideal in $\mathbb{F}_q[x]/(x^n-1)$ | theorem |
| DLP-based cryptography | discrete log in a cyclic group | $\langle g \rangle$ of prime order | hardness assumption |

The table is the honest version of the "single unified thread". The thread is
the **concept** of a cyclic group, not a chain of theorems connecting the four
rows.

### 5.2 What is genuinely connected

There is one direct chain, and it is number-theoretic:

```text
7 is a full reptend prime
  ⇒ ord_7(10) = 6
  ⇒ 1/7 = 0.142857142857...
  ⇒ 142857 is a cyclic number

GRH  ⇒  Artin's conjecture for a = 10  ⇒  infinitely many full reptend primes
```

The first block is definition and theorem. In the second block, Hooley's
theorem supplies the left-hand arrow: GRH implies the Artin density formula for
$a=10$. A positive predicted density implies infinitely many full reptend
primes; that is the right-hand arrow.

The FEC and cryptography rows are independent uses of cyclic groups in the
constructions discussed here:

- A cyclic code is an ideal in a group algebra; its cyclic group is the shift
  group $C_n$.
- A DLP system uses a cyclic subgroup of a finite field or an elliptic curve;
  its cyclic group is the subgroup generated by $g$.
- Neither construction needs full reptend primes, Artin's conjecture, or GRH.
- No result about full reptend primes, Artin's conjecture, or GRH implies
  anything about the FEC or cryptography rows. In particular, a proof of GRH
  would not change a Hamming decoder or break ECDLP.

The apparent unity comes from the fact that cyclic groups are ubiquitous. That
is a genuine mathematical theme, but it is not a theorem that "142857 explains
FEC and cryptography".

### 5.3 What would change

| Event | Effect on cyclic numbers | Effect on FEC | Effect on cryptography |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proof of classical RH | Not enough for Artin; GRH is stronger | None | None |
| Proof of GRH | Artin becomes unconditional; density established | None | None directly |
| Proof of Artin for $a=10$ | Infinitely many full reptend primes | None | None |
| Large fault-tolerant quantum computer | None | None | Breaks DH/ECC via Shor |
| New classical DLP algorithm | None | None | Breaks affected groups |

The table is deliberately blunt: the Riemann Hypothesis is a statement about
zeros of $L$-functions, not a master key to coding theory or cryptography.

```python
import itertools

import numpy as np

from modcode.fec.cyclic import cyclic_shift
from modcode.fec.hamming import Hamming74

code = Hamming74()
word = code.encode([1, 0, 1, 0])
assert np.array_equal(word, [1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1])

# Every cyclic shift of every codeword is again a codeword.
data = np.asarray(list(itertools.product((0, 1), repeat=4)), dtype=np.uint8)
codewords = {tuple(code.encode(bits).tolist()) for bits in data}
for candidate in codewords:
    for shift in range(7):
        rotated = cyclic_shift(np.asarray(candidate), shift)
        assert tuple(rotated.tolist()) in codewords
```

The set membership is the point: the rotated word must belong to the code, not
necessarily decode to the original four data bits. A cyclic shift of a
systematic codeword need not be systematic, so the data positions can move.
Invariance is a statement about the code as a set.

```python
from modcode.cryptography.toy_dh import ToyDiffieHellman

dh = ToyDiffieHellman(prime=23, generator=5)
alice, bob = 6, 15
assert dh.shared_secret(alice, dh.public_key(bob)) == dh.shared_secret(
    bob, dh.public_key(alice)
)
```

### 6.3 Natural extensions

- **Visualize repetends.** Plot the remainders $10^k \bmod p$ and the digits
  for $p=7,17,19$; verify that rotations correspond to $10^k \bmod p$.
- **Count, do not prove.** Compare the finite density of full reptend primes
  with $C_{\mathrm{Artin}}$ over increasing bounds. The fluctuation is part of
  the story; a finite count is not a proof of Artin.
- **Shorten and test.** Take Hamming(7,4), shorten it, and search for a
  rotation that leaves the shortened code. The existing test is a template.
- **Toy DLP.** For small $p$, brute-force the discrete logarithm and compare
  the cost with $\sqrt{p}$. This makes the security assumption concrete without
  pretending that a toy demonstrates security.
- **Define "semi-cyclic" before using it.** If a source uses the term, state
  the shift subgroup and the coordinates on which it acts. Otherwise the word
  is doing no mathematical work.

## 7. Claims audit

| Claim | Status | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| $142857$ is the repeating part of $1/7$ | true | $1/7 = 0.\overline{142857}$ |
| Its rotations are its first six multiples | true | theorem in §1.2 |
| $10$ is a primitive root modulo $7$ | true | $\mathrm{ord}_7(10)=6$ |
| Full reptend primes are those with $10$ a primitive root | true | for $p \nmid 10$ |
| "Deciding whether a given prime is full reptend is deep" | misleading | finite check; the infinitude is deep |
| Artin's conjecture is open for every specific $a$ | true | including $a=10$ |
| "RH implies Artin" | imprecise | GRH implies Artin; RH alone does not imply GRH |
| "Full reptend primes are infinite under RH" | imprecise | conditional on GRH (Hooley) |
| Hamming(7,4) is cyclic | true | with $g=x^3+x+1$ |
| "Semi-cyclic" is a standard code class | false | no single standard definition |
| "GRH underpins ECC security" | false | GRH is a tool, not a hardness assumption |
| A proof of RH would break DH/ECC | false | RH is not a hardness statement; Shor is the quantum threat |
| "QRH puts all zeros in the narrow strip $1/12 < \Re s < 11/12$" | imprecise | QRH is a zero-free half-plane $\Re s \le 11/12$ (later $7/8$); the interval follows from symmetry but is a **containing** strip, not a zero-free one |
| "QRH implies Artin's conjecture" | not established | Hooley needs GRH and its family/uniformity; QRH is a different, narrower claim |
| "QRH gives $P_b(x)=C_b x/\log x+O(x e^{-c\sqrt{\log x}})$" | false as stated | $C_b$ is Artin's conjecture; a $7/8$ half-plane gives a power-saving error, not that asymptotic |
| The four domains form one theorem chain | analogy | one genuine number-theory chain; shared vocabulary elsewhere |
